**Short answer:** I don't know of a published derivation of a₀ = c·H₀·Ω_m^φ. I believe it is not in the literature, though I can't rule out an obscure paper.

**Numerical check:** the stated value doesn't follow from the formula.
- c·H₀ = 3.0×10⁸ × 2.27×10⁻¹⁸ ≈ 6.8×10⁻¹⁰ m s⁻².
- 0.3^1.618 = exp(1.618 × ln 0.3) ≈ 0.143.
- The product is a₀ ≈ 0.97×10⁻¹⁰ m s⁻², not 1.2×10⁻¹⁰.

That is about 20% low, roughly 2σ or more from the measured value. The measured a₀ = 1.20±0.02 (random) ±0.24 (systematic) ×10⁻¹⁰ m s⁻² is from McGaugh, Lelli & Schombert, PRL 117, 201101 (2016). So the claim of a ≈1.2×10⁻¹⁰ result is not supported as written.

**What is known (related, not identical):**
- **a₀ ~ cH₀ coincidence:** Milgrom, ApJ 270, 365 (1983), and his later papers. Milgrom's "The modified dynamics as a vacuum effect," Phys. Lett. A 253, 273 (1999), gives a₀ ≈ cH₀/2π, which is about 1.08×10⁻¹⁰ for H₀ = 70. This matches the data better than the Ω_m^φ form.
- **Λ-based variants:** a₀ ~ c²√(Λ/3) = cH₀√Ω_Λ, and similar relations. These are discussed in Milgrom (1983, 1999) and reviewed in Famaey & McGaugh, Living Rev. Relativity 15, 10 (2012). I am unsure of the exact attribution for each variant.
- **Emergent gravity:** Verlinde, SciPost Phys. 2, 016 (2017), arXiv:1611.02269, obtains a₀ = cH₀/6 from de Sitter entropy. Ω_m-dependent versions are also discussed there.

**Assessment:** I know of no standard cosmological or gravitational argument that gives the golden ratio as an exponent on Ω_m. As stated, the construction looks like numerology, a fitted exponent with no derived mechanism. The program could be tested as follows:
1. Show a derivation that fixes the exponent φ.
2. Compare against the simpler cH₀/2π, which fits equally well or better with no free exponent.
3. Check redshift dependence. Ω_m and H change with z, so the formula predicts an evolving a₀, which can be compared with high-z rotation-curve constraints.
4. Note that the exponent is degenerate with the choice of Ω_m, H₀ and any O(1) prefactor. An exponent near 1.5 to 1.6 would be needed to reach 1.2×10⁻¹⁰, but that is a fit, not a derivation.

I would mark every citation above as needing verification of page and volume details before use. The Verlinde arXiv number and the Milgrom 1999 reference are the ones I'm most confident in.
