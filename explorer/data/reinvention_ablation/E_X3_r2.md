**Verdict:** Not viable as it stands. The arithmetic is consistent, but the construction is phenomenological, conflicts with existing data, and has no dynamical foundation.

**What checks out**
- 10⁻⁶ × 2π/H₀ ≈ 9×10⁴ yr is correct for H₀ ≈ 70 km/s/Mpc.
- The sign is right. Reducing m_eff at fixed gravitational force raises the acceleration.
- For a wide binary with M ≈ 1.5 M☉, P ≈ τ corresponds to a ≈ 3×10³ AU. That is where the claimed anomaly appears, and the boost to v is roughly 10–30% by ~10⁴ AU.

**Main weaknesses**
1. **No dynamics.** There is no action, Lagrangian, or field equation. The orbital period is a global, nonlocal property of a completed orbit, not a local quantity. It is undefined for unbound, eccentric, hierarchical (triple) or perturbed systems, and for a single body in a field. It is also unclear which body's period sets m_eff. Without a Lagrangian, energy and momentum conservation, and Lorentz and Galilean invariance, are not guaranteed. The equivalence principle also appears to be violated by construction.
2. **Solar-system constraints.** The correction scales as κP/τ for P ≪ τ. For Neptune (165 yr) it is about 6×10⁻⁴, for Jupiter about 4×10⁻⁵, and for Earth about 3×10⁻⁶. Kepler's third law would then fail between planets at levels far above ephemeris precision, which is ~10⁻⁹–10⁻¹⁰. Sedna-like orbits (P ~ 10⁴ yr) would show a few-percent boost. Long-period comets would be strongly affected.
3. **Wrong functional dependence for galaxies.** The log never saturates, so P ~ 2×10⁸ yr gives an acceleration boost of about 3.4×, and larger P gives more. Galaxy phenomenology (the radial acceleration relation, Tully–Fisher) depends on acceleration, not period, which is why MOND-like models use an acceleration scale. Systems with equal acceleration but different periods would be treated differently.
4. **Fine-tuning and cosmology.** The 10⁻⁶ factor and κ = 0.31 are free parameters, apparently tuned to the data. Tying τ to the present H₀ is unexplained, since H evolves.
5. **Empirical status of wide binaries.** The anomaly claims are disputed. Chae and Hernandez report boosts, while Banik et al. (2024) and others find Newtonian behavior once contamination from unresolved companions, and the data selection, are handled carefully. Fitting a disputed signal with a two-parameter form isn't a strong test.

**Strengths**
- It is explicit and falsifiable, and it makes a definite prediction for the velocity ratio versus P.
- It correctly reduces to Newton for short P, so laboratory tests and Earth–Moon tests are not trivially violated.

**What the authors should do next**
- Derive the law from an action, or from a nonlocal or memory-type effective theory. Specify how P is defined for general trajectories and how momentum and energy are conserved.
- Run a quantitative comparison against planetary ephemerides (INPOP, DE440), Sedna-like objects, long-period comets, and EP tests such as MICROSCOPE and LLR. Expect the solar-system bounds to force κ·τ⁻¹ to be tiny, or to rule out the form.
- Do a proper Bayesian fit of wide binaries with full treatment of hidden-companion contamination, and compare against Newtonian and MOND models.
- Test the galaxy-scale consequences and justify the ln form and the H₀ link, or drop them.
