## Pass 1: Casual Science Enthusiast

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 8 (landing, first-encounter, why-synchronism, honest-assessment, tools, coherence-explorer, galaxy-curve-plotter, tier-1, research-philosophy) + 2 attempted 404s
- **Understanding achieved**: partial
- **Would return?**: maybe
- **Overall impression**: Brave honesty and a few great analogies, but the front door has an equation on it and the guided tour seems to dead-end at step 1.

### Journey

I landed on the homepage and the first thing I noticed was the tagline — "What if one equation described reality from quantum to cosmic?" That's a hook I'd click on Reddit. The "Some predictions work. Some fail. One was refuted by external data" line under it was unusual. Most science sites lead with confidence. This one led with "we tried and we don't know yet." I liked that.

Then I saw the equation: `C(ρ) = tanh(γ · ln(ρ/ρcrit + 1))`. My eyes did the thing they always do — slid right past it. I have no idea what tanh is. I sort of remember ln from high school but I couldn't tell you what it does. I felt a little embarrassed and clicked "First Encounter" because that's where they directed "new visitors."

First Encounter was a relief. "Coherence is how collectively a group of things behaves. A marching band moving in lockstep: high coherence." That I get. A marching band vs. a wandering crowd — perfect analogy, I'll remember it. They said "Step 1 of 7." Great, I'm in a tour. But when I tried to guess the URL for step 2 or step 3 (because the "Next" button isn't clickable via the way I'm browsing), I got 404s on `/first-encounter/2`, `/first-encounter/step-3`, `/first-encounter/density`. I gave up and went to the top nav.

"Why Synchronism?" started promising — "Physics has a fragmentation problem." OK, I've heard physicists complain about this. But then the equation came back, with no walkthrough. I bounced.

"Honest Assessment" is where the site won me back. "0 confirmed predictions, 1 refuted by external data." That's striking. The page actually goes through what failed (superconductor temperature off by 6.5×, melting points off by 53%, the DESI cosmology test going the wrong way). I don't know what DESI is but the phrase "the predicted effect went the wrong direction" lands hard. I respect this.

I went to Tools because I wanted to do something rather than read. The Coherence Explorer described sliders for γ and ρcrit. I couldn't actually use the sliders (the interactive UI didn't render for me) but the on-page text told me what to try: "Move γ from 2.0 downward and watch the curve flatten." That's friendly. The Galaxy Curve Plotter was the highlight — it explained dark matter in one sentence ("the outer stars rotate just as fast as the inner ones") and shows three competing theories (dark matter, MOND, Synchronism) against the same data. Even though the page admits the framework's prediction "failed the stricter quantitative check," I appreciated that the tool let me see what failing looks like.

Tier 1 was mostly tables and prediction statements. I caught the part where TEST-04a is labeled "FAILED" but the rest had terms like "fσ8" and "DESI" that I'd need to Google.

Research Philosophy went too far for my taste. "47 of 3,308 sessions produced genuinely novel predictions" — and then "0 of 6 retained after expert audit." After enough of this I started wondering why I'm still on the site. The honesty is admirable but it also raised the question: if nothing has worked, what am I supposed to take away from this visit?

## Pass 2: Technical Writer

### Journey

I started with the top-nav inventory because that's where IA either works or doesn't. Eight items, no submenus, no overflow — clean. Two parallel CTAs on the landing ("Start Exploring", "See What Failed") plus a persona-routed paragraph ("New visitors → First Encounter / Physicists → Key Claims or Honest Assessment"). The persona-routing is well-intended but Pass 1's enthusiast was right to flag it: "Physicists" reads as a credential, not an audience tag. "Familiar with mean-field theory" would route on knowledge, not identity.

I dropped into `/first-encounter` to test the tour mechanic, since the landing copy promises "Step 1 of 7." Step 1 shows that label — and a **Previous** button pointing to `/two-reframes`. I followed it. `/two-reframes` has no step indicator at all and its own Next/Previous pair (`/observer-problem` ↔ `/measurement-without-observers`). So either the tour is actually longer than 7 and Step 1 is mislabeled, or Previous on Step 1 shouldn't exist, or there are two overlapping tours and the breadcrumb doesn't disambiguate them. Whichever it is, the navigation contract is broken: the labels and the links don't tell the same story.

I went to `/learning-paths` next, expecting a sitemap of the tour. It does offer three difficulty tracks (Beginner/Intermediate/Advanced) — a nice IA pattern — but First Encounter is listed as a single node in the Beginner track, not as 7 enumerated steps with deep links. A reader who knows they want "the step about decoherence" has no way to navigate there directly; they have to click Next until they find it.

The glossary was the highlight of the audit. ~46 entries, all defined, almost all cross-linked to dedicated pages. **tanh is defined.** Every validation badge is defined. DESI, BAO, fσ₈, SPARC, MOND, A2ACW, NP2, BTFR, η — all there. As a stand-alone reference document, the glossary is the strongest artifact on the site. The problem is that it's the *only* place these terms get expanded. On `/honest-assessment`, `/test-catalog`, `/key-claims`, and `/two-reframes`, acronyms appear naked on first use. Pass 1 hit this too. Standard tech-writing convention is parenthetical expansion at first mention per page, then acronym thereafter. The site has the definitions; it just isn't surfacing them at point-of-use.

`/honest-assessment` was a pleasant correction to my Pass 1 expectation. The validation-badge legend is **at the top of the page, before any badge is used**. Six states defined cleanly: Validated, Strongly Supported, Untested, Speculative, Reparametrization, Failed. This is exactly how a legend should work. The problem is downstream: `/test-catalog` does *not* show badges next to its 24 proposed experiments, and `/key-claims` shows only 3 numbered claims (Untested, Speculative, Failed) — so a reader scanning either page can't tell at a glance which entries the framework actually owns. The taxonomy exists, but it's applied inconsistently.

Test numbering has the same problem. The roadmap header says "24 Proposed Experiments" — none of which are labeled TEST-01 through TEST-24 on that page. Yet other pages (and the public-facing /tier-1-existing) reference specific tests as "TEST-03" and "TEST-04a." There is no anchor to land on when chasing a reference. The test catalog is acting as a tier overview and as a numbered registry at the same time, and the numbering isn't winning.

I tested two interactive tools different from Pass 1's. The **γ Calculator** is a model of how to handle uncertainty in a tool's framing: it opens with two upfront caveats ("γ lacks rigorous derivation; preset Ncorr values are back-fits, not measurements"), then lets you compute anyway. Inputs, presets, and outputs are labeled. The four "regime labels" (Weakly Correlated, Boundary, Strongly Correlated, Collective) aren't defined on the page itself, but the link path to `/coherence-function` is there. Acceptable trade-off — though a one-line tooltip on each label would close the gap.

The **Equation Walkthrough** was the genuine surprise of the audit. Six interactive steps, each with explanatory prose, and the tanh explanation on this page is **excellent**: *"Hyperbolic tangent — an S-shaped saturation function ... like a dimmer switch: input very negative → output near 0; input very large → output near 1."* This is exactly the gloss Pass 1's enthusiast was asking for. But it lives three clicks from the landing-page equation that triggered the question. The dimmer-switch line should be a hover-tooltip on the landing-page `tanh` itself.

I closed by checking `/mrh`. MRH is one of the most-referenced acronyms across the site, with its own dedicated page that expands it correctly ("MRH: Markov Relevancy Horizon") and has a Core Theory breadcrumb. But the page isn't reachable from the top nav — it's only an in-body cross-link. For a term used this often, that's underweighted in the IA.

## Pass 3: Graduate Physics Student

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 13 successful (key-claims, coherence-function, parameter-derivations, honest-assessment, equation-walkthrough, galaxy-curve-plotter, tier-1-existing, mrh, consciousness-threshold, test-catalog, born-rule, two-reframes, coherence-explorer) + 4 attempted 404s (phase-diagram, critical-exponents, coherence-comparison, consciousness-threshold-demo)
- **Understanding achieved**: solid (on the framework's actual structure and limitations; the framework's own internal audit is doing most of the work)

- **Overall impression**: Extraordinarily epistemically honest at the self-audit layer, but the front-of-site is still using order-parameter / phase-transition / "derivation" / "critical" vocabulary for objects that the site itself, deeper in, correctly identifies as a phenomenological compander with no critical behavior, a reparametrization, or an operationally undefined boundary.

### Journey

I went straight to the theory pages. The very first thing I wanted to know is whether `C(ρ) = tanh(γ · ln(ρ/ρ_crit + 1))` is derived or fit. The Coherence Function page answered cleanly and to its credit: *"the tanh shape is motivated by mean-field theory, not derived from it … C(ρ) is a phenomenological ansatz."* And further: *"any sigmoid satisfying the four constraints (logistic, erf, arctan, Hill) would have been an equally valid choice. tanh is the most natural choice given the Landau analogy, but not the uniquely forced one."* That is exactly the right caveat. As a condensed-matter person I would have written almost the same sentence.

But the front-of-site keeps doing something the deeper pages refuse to do: it calls this thing a phase transition with a critical density. I opened the Coherence Explorer at the default settings (γ=2, ρ_crit=1.00) and the on-page readout is `C(ρ_crit) = 0.8824`. That is not a critical density. The function is past its inflection by then. The argument γ·ln(ρ/ρ_crit + 1) is zero when ρ=0 (not at ρ_crit), and tanh's steepest slope is at its argument zero. So in this parametrization the steepest-slope point is at the **left boundary**, not at ρ_crit, and not at C=0.5. None of the standard Landau machinery applies — there is no order parameter sitting on a free-energy minimum, no symmetry-breaking, no susceptibility divergence at ρ_crit. ρ_crit is the saturation knee of a μ-law / Naka-Rushton compressive nonlinearity. That should be its name. The Coherence Explorer in particular displays the 0.8824 number with no acknowledgement that this contradicts the "critical density" reading.

I tried to open `/phase-diagram` and `/critical-exponents` next — both 404. The Honest Assessment page lists "Critical exponents (2× off)" under Failed, but you can't navigate to the page that would let you see what was predicted vs. measured. A 2× discrepancy on a critical exponent isn't really a "we missed the calibration"; it's evidence the system doesn't live in the universality class the framework is comparing it to — i.e., the prediction probably failed because the framework doesn't have critical behavior to begin with, not because γ was tuned wrong. This is also the right diagnosis for the "Reparametrization" verdict the site keeps issuing on individual results: the compander C(ρ) is doing all the work, and any other monotonic saturating function would have produced the same fits. Without a `/coherence-comparison` page (also 404) running AIC/BIC across tanh vs. logistic vs. Hill vs. erf vs. Naka-Rushton on the same SPARC residuals, the framework can't even tell which sigmoid the data actually prefers — and it can't claim tanh is doing the work rather than the four monotonicity constraints alone.

Born Rule was the clearest case where the site labels itself correctly. Step 3 ("the only consistent probability measure is |α|²") is asserted, not proved — it is not Gleason, not Zurek envariance with the full derivation, just a closure assertion. The page itself owns this: *"Whether this is truly more fundamental than the Born rule itself, or just a reformulation at the same level, is debatable — and the argument does not predict any deviation from |α|² in any regime."* That is the correct verdict. My only objection is the word "derivation" in the page title — for a reparametrization, "Born rule from coherence: an equivalent formulation" would be more honest than "Born Rule Derivation," and would match the page's actual content.

MRH was the one that worried me most. The page itself admits *"The current MRH framework specifies operational criteria (predictive sufficiency, predictive closure) but not a probability distribution, integration measure, graphical model, or explicit mapping"* and suggests renaming to "Relevance Horizon" since the "Markov" label sets expectations the construct doesn't meet. Right. A Markov blanket has a concrete definition: the parents, children, and parents-of-children in a graphical model, or equivalently the minimal conditioning set that makes a node conditionally independent of the rest. Without that structure, calling a horizon "Markov" is a category claim the construct can't cash. The site has already diagnosed this; it should act on it.

The Tier 1 page was sobering and well-written. TEST-04a is correctly classified as a "mechanism-class failure" — the sign of the leading-order suppression is reversed (predicted fσ₈ ≈ 0.418, measured ≈ 0.55 ± 0.06). That kind of failure isn't a calibration miss; it kills the whole "suppression-of-growth" mechanism class, which is exactly the right reading. The page also notes Session 107 was committed 2025-12-10 while DESI DR1 published April 2024 — i.e., the "prediction" was registered ~18 months after the data. Pre-registration audit acknowledges this. Good. TEST-09 (BTFR slope n=3.85±0.09) is *consistent* with the framework's regime-dependent prediction but is also consistent with plain MOND — so it can't discriminate. The framework correctly flags this as MOND-degenerate. Same for TEST-10 (DM fraction in dwarfs). None of the Tier 1 list represents a prospective falsification yet. The site says so.

Consciousness Threshold was almost good. The page is candid that the eight convergent C≈0.50 numbers are Synchronism-internal cross-checks, not independent empirical measurements, and the convergence is a self-consistency check, not corroboration. Right verdict. The math problem I noted above (steepest-slope point of tanh-of-log is at ρ=0, not at C=0.5) however means even the *internal* claim that "C=0.5 corresponds to the steepest-slope regime" isn't true in this parametrization. The page doesn't claim consciousness emerges at the inflection of C(ρ); it claims it emerges at the inflection of f(γ,D,S), which is a different sigmoid. Fine — but then f is being silently equipped with a different shape than C(ρ), and the framework has two distinct compander-class functions wearing the same letter.

I tried Galaxy Curve Plotter as my second interactive tool (Pass 1 already touched it casually; I wanted to evaluate the actual physics statement). The page admits *"All three fit the observations; none is confirmed over the others by rotation curve data alone"* and explicitly does not declare a winner — and then concedes the stricter scatter test (TEST-03, R²=0.14 against 20% kill) was triggered as Failed. That is exactly how a researcher should write the page. The Synchronism curve "nearly overlaps" MOND on the qualitative shape — which is precisely what you would expect from any saturation function calibrated to the same a₀-scale, because the data has very little discriminating power between sigmoid families. The page draws this conclusion honestly.

### Journey

What I could *not* find on the public A2ACW page is any counterfactual test of the protocol itself. The page acknowledges A2ACW cannot do out-of-distribution evaluation but doesn't quantify its in-distribution failure mode. The 1.4% "novel-claim survival rate" is offered as an upper bound but it isn't paired with a baseline — what fraction of *known* physics that the model has been trained on would the same protocol flag as novel?
