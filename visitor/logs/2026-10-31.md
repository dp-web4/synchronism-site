## Pass 1: Casual Science Enthusiast

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 10 (landing, /why-synchronism, /first-encounter, /coherence-explorer, /galaxy-plotter, /gamma-calculator, /honest-assessment, /learning-paths, /what-synchronism-is-not, /glossary)
- **Understanding achieved**: partial
- **Would return?**: maybe
- **Overall impression**: The honesty pulled me in, but almost every page drops an alphabet soup on me (MOND, BTFR, RAR, SPARC, ΔBIC). After ~40 minutes I know *that* it failed, but I only half-understand *what* it was trying to do.

### Journey
I came from Reddit. The landing headline, "What if one equation described reality from quantum to cosmic?", is classic clickbait for someone like me, and then the next line undercuts it: *"We proposed this. We tested it. It did not hold as stated."* I've never seen a site do that. My first reaction was "is this a joke?" My second was "OK, I trust these people more than the usual theory-of-everything guy." The "built by AI agents with one human lead" line also made me curious.

Then the landing page hits me with a bullet list I can't parse. "Galaxy tests used related functions keyed to acceleration, not density." "The one non-degenerate galaxy test reduced the framework to MOND." "Six refutations… 3 to 8 under other rules." "0 of 9 audited claims surviving, but the audit instrument is uncalibrated." I don't know what "non-degenerate" means. I don't know why the failure count is a range. I don't know what an "audit instrument" is. The scoreboard names things like "BTFR slope," "RAR shape," and "Cassini/SPARC squeeze." To me, that's a list of failures in a foreign language. I nearly bounced. What saved it was the clear "Start here: Why Synchronism? (~8 min)" button and the audience routes ("Beginner Path for non-physicists").

**Why Synchronism?** The opening is good: "Physics has a fragmentation problem," then crowd vs. marching band, and a dimmer switch. The "What happened, in three lines" box was the best thing I read all session: one equation for how "crowding" makes parts act as one, tested, and it lost where it differed from existing physics. I liked the "stop here" option. But past the fold, even with the technical part folded away, the results section throws in Tully–Fisher, R², a₀, H₀, Hill/Naka–Rushton, Abrikosov–Gor'kov and YBCO. Those are words I'll never look up.

**First Encounter** has more analogies (marching band, whisper in a loud room, neutron star vs. lone electron), and I liked them. But the formula appears with "tanh" and "ln". The page calls tanh an "S-shaped curve" but never says what it *is*, and I don't know what a natural log is. Then: "C measures density-driven collective ordering, not quantum phase coherence — quantum systems like superconductors land at low C." This confused me more than anything else. I thought superconductors were the textbook example of everything acting together. If the "coherence" score says a superconductor *isn't* coherent, then what is the score measuring? I think the site is telling me this is one of the problems. I couldn't tell whether that's a confession or a definition.

I also hit "predicted correlation +0.55, measured −0.55." I get that the sign was backwards, which is kind of a great failure story, but I don't know what was being correlated.

**Coherence Explorer (tool 1).** WebFetch can't drag sliders, so I read the controls, defaults and the instruction text. The instruction is exactly what a beginner needs: "first drag γ up to 2 (the original guess), then drag it back down to 0.5 and watch the S flatten into a gentle ramp." Presets are labelled. The key values at the default (C = 0.33 / 0.83 / 0.98 at 1×, 10× and 100× the reference density) are concrete. A dashed line at C = 0.5 and "sparse"/"dense" labels help. Confusions: one preset is called "γ = ½ — same curve as MOND's formula," and I don't understand how a coherence curve can *be* a gravity formula. There's also a note that ρcrit "is not the curve's midpoint," which raises the question of why it's called "critical."

**Galaxy Curve Plotter (tool 2).** This is the page where it finally clicked. Blue dots for "what telescopes measure," a gray line for "Newton, visible matter only," violet for "this framework's equation (tested reading, failed)," and green for "MOND, the rival theory." The plain story: the gray line drops at the edges, the real stars don't, and that gap is the dark matter puzzle. MOND closes it with one constant. Their equation has the wrong shape. That's a picture I could explain to a friend. One small wobble: the caveats list says the far-out agreement is "partly built in" because Vflat is fed in from existing fits. That makes me wonder how much of *any* line matching is real, and the page doesn't say in plain words how to read that. Five galaxies with real data (since 2026-10-26) feels trustworthy.

**γ Calculator (tool 3).** The page is self-labelled "a γ formula kept to show how it fails," and it opens with "This tool lets you explore how a failed formula fails." That's charming and weird. Clicking the presets (based on the description) teaches the lesson: ideal gas gets the steepest switch and BEC/superconductor get nearly flat curves, which the page says is backwards. I *think* I get it: the formula says "more particles acting together → gentler transition," while reality says the opposite. But I needed to read it three times, and "Ncorr" is only loosely defined. Why would a site keep a broken calculator in its toolbox? Probably for the lesson, but a beginner isn't told that until after clicking.

**Learning Paths** helped. The Beginner path is 5 steps and about 37 minutes, and it says "No physics background needed." The time estimates are nice. But it never says what I'll *understand* at the end. Step 5 is "read only the box" on Honest Assessment, and the full page is apparently 80 minutes. There's a missing space typo: "Separately,1 of the 7 paths."

**Honest Assessment.** I only read the "If you read nothing else" box, as instructed. The first bullet: "The one equation failed its galaxy tests. Where it fits, it is MOND in different notation. As a theory of galaxies: fatal." That's blunt and clear, and I respect it. Then I scrolled the headings and found "RAR Transition Shape — SPARC ΔBIC=+184 (points as independent; ≈ +11 by galaxy), γ=2 Disfavoured, γfree=MOND (2026-05-21)." That heading alone is a wall. Telling me the page is ~18,000 words is honest, and also terrifying.

**What Synchronism Is Not.** It's easy to skim, with bold lead-ins. "Not journal-reviewed — checked by AI models reviewing each other" is a big deal and I'm glad it's said plainly. But it calls MOND "the rival gravity idea" with "40 years of empirical success" and **never explains what MOND actually does**. MOND is the hero of the whole site's failure story (the framework "reduces to MOND"), so this is the one word I most needed defined in-line.

**Glossary.** The MOND entry opens accessibly ("instead of adding invisible matter to explain why galaxies spin too fast at their edges…") but then drifts into "baryonic Tully–Fisher relation" and "CMB." "Retrodiction: counts as consistency, never as confirmation" is perfect. 122 terms with an A–Z index is nice, but I found no search box in what I saw.

### Tools Tested
- **Coherence Explorer**: worked (I read the controls and outputs; WebFetch can't drag sliders). Intuitive. The "drag γ to 2, then back to 0.5" instruction is the best beginner guidance on the site. The "γ = ½ = MOND" preset label confused me.
- **Galaxy Curve Plotter**: worked. Mostly intuitive, and it's the moment the whole site made sense. The colour legend in plain words ("what telescopes measure") is excellent. The caveat about built-in agreement was unclear.
- **γ Calculator**: worked. Confusing at first: a tool built to demonstrate a broken formula. Once understood, the "superconductor gets the flattest curve, which is backwards" lesson is memorable.

### Unanswered Questions
1. What does MOND actually *do*, in one sentence? Everything on this site is compared to it.
2. If the coherence score rates superconductors low, what real-world thing does a *high* score correspond to? Neutron stars? Galaxies? Is a high score good or bad?
3. Why is the number of failures a range (3–8)? Who decides which counting rule is "current"?
4. If the equation lost, why does the site still exist? Is the project dead, or is something still being tested? I'd like one sentence on "what's still alive."
5. ~3,300 AI sessions and no outside physicist: has anyone outside actually looked at this? Can I?

### Verdict
This is the most honest science website I've seen. It leads with its own failure, and the Galaxy Curve Plotter gave me a real "aha" about the dark matter puzzle that I didn't have before. But the beginner experience is still a minefield of acronyms. MOND, the word the whole story hinges on, never gets a plain one-line explanation where I need it. I'd share it on Reddit as "cool example of science admitting it was wrong," but I couldn't tell a friend what the equation was *for* beyond "crowds vs. marching bands."

### Journey
**Landing.** The nav is flat with 10 items, no dropdowns. "Start Here" and the hero's "Beginner Path" both go to /why-synchronism, and the Start Here tooltip says so ("step 1 of the Beginner Path"). That's consistent. An "Abbreviations ahead (RAR, ΔBIC, BTFR, Ωm, Ncorr, CFD, SPARC): short key, or the glossary" line is a good pattern. It warns rather than expands, which is acceptable for a landing page. MOND and MRH are expanded inline. I checked all seven #anchors the landing page links to on /honest-assessment, plus /glossary#tanh, against the served HTML, and every one exists.

**Glossary.** This is the strongest IA artifact on the site. An eight-row "How to read labels on this site" table separates verdicts (two badge families) from operational states, lifecycle verbs, descriptors, and tool Kind and Level. It even says "If a label is not in the first two rows, it is not a badge." That rule is exactly what a reader needs. Defects:
- The first row's "Question it answers" cell repeats itself: "Where does this claim sit relative to current research focus? (where the claim sits relative to current research focus (MRH = …))". It looks like a template field rendered twice, with nested parentheses.
- The intro prose says "Deprecated badges (Validated, Strongly Supported)". The table row directly below is titled **Retired** label and lists **three** members (Validated, Strongly Supported, MOND-shared). Honest Assessment's badge section says "Retired, still met on a few older pages: Validated, Strongly Supported, MOND-shared". A few lines later it says "plus deprecated back-compat tags". That's two words for one category, and member lists of different lengths.
- The "Descriptor / qualifier" row contains "(Underpowered as Registered is a lifecycle verb only, since 2026-10-23)". A table cell that lists what *isn't* in the row is a history note in a definition slot. The lifecycle row spells the same term "Underpowered as registered" (lowercase r).

**Badge definitions (/honest-assessment#validation-badge-definitions).** The nine-badge table, one line each, is exactly right. But **Audited-Negative** ("closed audit finding; durable; does not move") sits in the *MRH-relationship* family, which the glossary describes as "where a claim sits in the research inventory", not a verdict. To a reader, "Audited-Negative" reads like a verdict, and it's used as one on tool cards ("This formula is audited-negative; the calculator shows why"). The family boundary is fuzzier than the "only the first two rows are verdicts" rule implies.

**MRH, the word.** /mrh's own page argues the name "promises a specific mathematical structure" that "is not currently met" and suggests renaming it to "Relevance Horizon". The glossary separately explains that the *badge family* "MRH-relationship" borrows the term as a metaphor and "say[s] nothing about the Markov Relevancy Horizon as a physics concept." So one acronym names (a) a physics concept the site says is mis-named, and (b) a badge family the site says is unrelated to (a).

### Summary

### Journey
My colleague said "interesting AI project". The landing page tells me in the first screen that the equation "did not hold as stated", with 0 confirmed predictions and 6 refutations. So I skipped persuasion and went to check the algebra.

**/parameter-derivations.** The page opens by saying it isn't a derivations page ("Parameter Calibration & Honest Ansätze"), which is correct. I checked:
- tanh(γ ln(1+x)) = [(1+x)^{2γ} − 1]/[(1+x)^{2γ} + 1] follows from tanh u = (e^{2u}−1)/(e^{2u}+1). ✓
- Small-x limit C ≈ γx and large-x limit 1 − C ≈ 2x^{−2γ}. ✓
- At γ = ½, C = x/(x+2) = μ_simple(x/2), so MOND's simple μ with a₀ → 2a₀. ✓ This is an exact identity, and the page badges it Reparametrization, the right call.
- A = 4π/(β_J² G R₀²) with G = 4.301×10⁻³ pc (km/s)²/M☉ and R₀ = 8000 pc gives 4.565×10⁻⁵ M☉ pc⁻³ (km/s)⁻², and 0.0294/4.565×10⁻⁵ = 644. ✓ The "644× unit conversion" really is a fudge, as the page says.

**Globular clusters: the statistic I'd reject.** /honest-assessment's GC section reports a "ranking re-score (2026-10-30): cluster bootstrap on paired per-cluster differences". Work it through. Per cluster, residual_A − residual_B = (s_obs − s_A) − (s_obs − s_B) = s_B − s_A. **The observed outer slope cancels.** The data enter only through error weights and cluster selection. So a cluster bootstrap on that difference measures how *consistently the two models predict different slopes* across 42 mass models, not how strongly the data prefer one. Two models that both miss the data badly, by different amounts, will show many-σ "separations". The "identity control reproduces every row to ±0.001" checks the bookkeeping and can't catch this. The honest data-facing comparison is each row's residual against its *own* error: Newton −0.057 ± 0.027 versus MOND+EFE −0.093 ± 0.027 is ~1σ apart, not 8σ. If the site wants a single place where "density/coherence-keyed MOND" is a live research program with discriminating predictions, that's the comparator to position against.
